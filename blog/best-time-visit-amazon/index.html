Best Time to Visit the Amazon:
A Month-by-Month Guide

Edson Nogueira, Lead Naturalist June 2026 7 min read

The Amazon has two seasons, not four: high water (roughly December–June) and low water (July–November). There is no bad time to visit — each season offers different experiences. The dry season gives better terrestrial wildlife and exposed beaches; the wet season gives flooded forest accessible by canoe and more active river dolphins.

Two seasons, not a "best" one

Most travel articles describe the Amazon dry season as simply "better." This is a simplification that costs travellers the most extraordinary experience the Amazon offers — the flooded forest.

When the Amazon rises 10 to 15 metres between December and June, the forest floods. Trees stand in several metres of black water. You navigate between their trunks by canoe. Fish eat fruit that falls from the canopy. Dolphins hunt through the submerged understorey. It is one of the most unusual ecosystems on earth, and it is only accessible during the high-water season.

Equally, the low-water season (July–November) concentrates wildlife in ways the wet season does not. As rivers fall, fish crowd into shrinking pools, caimans line exposed sandbanks, and terrestrial mammals converge at waterholes. Beach-nesting river turtles lay eggs on the white sand beaches of the Rio Negro. Trails become accessible through terrain that would be flooded six months earlier.

The question is not which season is better — it's which season is better for what you want.

☀️

Dry Season

July – November

  • Exposed beaches and sandbanks
  • Wildlife concentrates at river edges
  • Best fishing (piranhas, catfish)
  • Clearer trails — less flooding
  • River turtle nesting beaches
  • Peak birdwatching (migrants arrive)
  • Lower humidity, more sun
🌧️

Wet Season

December – June

  • Flooded igapó forest by canoe
  • Pink dolphins most active
  • Extraordinary flooded-forest birdlife
  • Monkeys feeding in flooded canopy
  • Fewer other tourists
  • Lush, green, dramatically different
  • Shorter daily rain bursts (not all-day)

On the Rio Negro: Rain in the Amazon is almost always short and intense — rarely does it rain all day. Most tours continue normally through rain. The Rio Negro's dark, acidic water also produces significantly fewer mosquitoes than white-water rivers. Many guests are surprised by how manageable conditions are year-round.

Month-by-month breakdown

The transition between seasons is gradual rather than sharp. Here is what each month typically offers for travellers departing from Manaus:

Month Season What to expect
January High water Water rising fast. Flooded forest accessible. Dolphins very active in igapó channels. Bird breeding season underway. Fewer tourists.
February High water Continuing rise. Carnival month — city lively, tour availability may be tighter. Flooded forest at its most dramatic.
March High water Near peak flood. Some of the deepest igapó penetration possible by canoe. Orchids in bloom. Green everywhere.
April High water Peak water or just beginning to recede. Strong flooded-forest access. Good for dolphins and fishing-eagle sightings.
May Transition Water beginning to fall. Mix of both seasons — beaches starting to emerge. Scarlet macaws peak near clay licks. Good overall.
June Transition Water falling noticeably. Beaches appearing on Rio Negro. Both igapó canoe trips and trail walks possible. Excellent month.
July Low water Low season begins. Beaches open. Piranhas concentrate in shrinking pools — fishing excellent. Caimans visible on sandbanks. Drier days.
August Low water Peak low water. White-sand beaches on Rio Negro. Best for terrestrial mammals, caiman, and fishing. River turtle nesting begins.
September Low water Excellent birdwatching — Austral migrants arriving from the south. Clear trails. Caimans on beaches nightly. Strong wildlife month overall.
October Low water Wildlife concentrated at river edges. Catfish and arapaima fishing peaks. Turtle hatchlings emerging on beaches. Hot and dry.
November Transition First rains returning. Still largely low-water conditions. Good wildlife access before flooding begins. Boreal migrants from North America arriving.
December Rising water Flooding begins. Some beaches still present early in month. Bird breeding season starts again. Holiday period — book well ahead.

Wildlife by season

Dry season: July–November

The low-water season concentrates animal activity in predictable ways. Fish crowd into lakes and pools that were spread across the flooded forest weeks earlier. Caimans follow — spending long days on exposed sandbanks, and patrolling river edges at night. Terrestrial mammals that were dispersed through the flooded forest now appear along river banks at dawn and dusk.

This is also peak season for the Rio Negro's extraordinary birdlife. Austral migrants from southern South America join the resident species in September and October. Boreal migrants from North America and Europe appear from November onwards. A well-guided birdwatching tour in September or October can record 100+ species in five days without difficulty.

Our 7-day Birdwatching tour operates year-round but performs best in this window.

Wet season: December–June

The high-water season transforms access to the forest. The igapó — seasonally flooded black-water forest — fills with several metres of water, and the entire structure of the ecosystem changes. The forest becomes a river. You canoe between tree trunks, watching for species that would be invisible from a trail.

Pink river dolphins are measurably more active during the high-water season, when prey fish disperse through the flooded forest and dolphins follow them. You encounter them in channels you're navigating rather than from a boat on open water — a qualitatively different experience. Fruiting trees in the flooded canopy attract large monkey troops that are difficult to see at other times of year.

Rainfall is intense but short — typically one to two hours in the afternoon, then clear again. It does not prevent activities. Night walks and caiman searches operate normally. The forest is louder, greener, and more dramatically alive than during the dry season.

"The flooded Amazon is not a degraded version of the dry-season forest. It's a different ecosystem entirely — one that most visitors never see because they follow the conventional advice to come in the dry season."

What about May, June, and November?

The transition months are often overlooked and frequently offer the best overall experience. In May and June, water is falling — beaches are beginning to emerge while some igapó is still navigable by canoe. You get elements of both seasons. June in particular is a genuinely excellent month: dry weather, the forest still green from the rains, beaches appearing, and wildlife beginning to concentrate.

November is the inverse: the last of the dry-season beach conditions, with the first rains returning. Migrant birds are passing through. Wildlife is still concentrated. It's often quieter and less expensive than peak months.

Our recommendation

First-time visitors with no strong preference: July to October. The dry season delivers consistently good wildlife density and the logistical comfort of less rain. The experience aligns most closely with what people picture when they imagine an "Amazon tour."

Returning visitors, or those specifically interested in flooded forest: March to May. The flooded igapó is one of the most extraordinary natural environments accessible from Manaus. If you've already done a dry-season tour, the wet-season flooded forest is a completely different trip.

Birdwatchers: September and October for maximum species diversity, including both Austral and Boreal migrants alongside the full resident complement.
